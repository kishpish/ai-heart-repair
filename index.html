A second beat for the broken heart

AI-driven inverse design of bio-instructive hydrogels for post-infarction myocardial regeneration

About 805,000 people in the United States have a heart attack every year, and the heart muscle they lose does not grow back. I am working toward a therapy that changes that: an injectable hydrogel designed for one patient’s heart, which supports the damaged wall straight away and could one day carry the cells or growth factors that help lost muscle regrow.

This project builds the engine for it. Starting from a single cardiac MRI, it reconstructs a working model of the patient’s heart, searches ten million gel formulations with a purpose-built AI model, and tests its design ranking against full physics simulation. A therapy that helps the heart repair itself could change the outlook for millions of people, and this is a first step toward designing one.

Krishiv Potluri, independent researcher in Austin, Texas

01

The heart does not repair itself

  1. A working ventricle

    This is the left ventricle, the heart’s main pumping chamber, seen in cross-section the way a short-axis MRI slice shows it. With every beat its wall thickens and the cavity shrinks, which is what pushes blood out to the body. Relaxed between beats, healthy muscle is soft, with a stiffness of 8 to 15 kPa. §1.1

  2. A heart attack kills part of the wall

    When a coronary artery is blocked, the muscle it feeds is starved of oxygen and dies. Collagen scar grows in its place and stiffens to at least 55 kPa, and because scar cannot contract, that stretch of wall stops helping the heart pump. This happens to about 805,000 people in the United States each year. §1.1 §2.11 IEEE §I

  3. The muscle does not grow back

    The adult heart renews less than one percent of its muscle cells each year, which means the loss is effectively permanent. Today’s drugs can slow what follows, but they cannot replace the muscle that is gone. §1.1, citing Bergmann et al., 2009

  4. Then the damage feeds itself

    Over time the scar thins and the chamber stretches. The law of Laplace shows why that is dangerous: stress in the wall rises with the chamber’s radius and falls with the wall’s thickness, so both changes load the surviving muscle more heavily, and that extra load drives further stretching.

    σ = P r2 h

    The cycle reinforces itself, and within a year 20 to 35 percent of survivors have developed heart failure. §1.1, citing Jenča et al., 2021

  5. A promising answer: thicken the wall

    If a soft gel is injected into the weakened wall, the wall becomes thicker, and the same law predicts that the stress on it should fall. An early simulation study found that filling just 4.5 percent of the wall’s volume lowered fibre stress in the border zone by 17 percent, from 28.2 to 23.3 kPa. §1.2, citing Wall et al., 2006

    Hydrogels can also do more than brace the wall. They can conduct electricity, break down on a chosen schedule and present cells with a particular stiffness, which makes them a natural starting point for therapies that aim at repair. §1.2 §12.3

  6. What has been missing

    Gels like these have already been delivered to patients, yet the results have been mixed, and a randomized trial of a bioabsorbable matrix delivered through the coronary artery did not reduce remodeling compared with placebo. §1.2, PRESERVATION-1

    Three gaps help explain why. Heart models that a physics solver can use are still not produced routinely from patient data without manual work, injection sites are chosen by eye with no mechanical rationale, and the space of possible formulations is far too large to explore one simulation at a time. §1.3

    This project was built to close all three.

02

From one MRI to a gel designed for that heart

The pipeline runs in six stages. The first five take a patient’s scan to a personalized gel design, and the sixth tests the design ranking against full physics simulation, with every prediction sealed before the simulations ran. Select a stage to see how it works.

Image to model

The starting point is a stack of short-axis MRI slices on which an expert has traced the inner and outer walls of the heart. From those sparse outlines, eight automated steps build a watertight surface and then a solid mesh of tetrahedra that a physics solver can work with, and at no point does anyone edit the mesh by hand.

Built
10 patient hearts, with 3,658,183 elements and 677,557 nodes in total
Check
Zero inverted elements in every mesh
Time
About fifteen minutes per patient

§4.4

For specialists

Cubic Hermite atlas fits of roughly sixty nodes, in prolate spheroidal coordinates, are evaluated to a point cloud and triangulated, then refined by three rounds of Loop subdivision, smoothed, and passed through a seven-operator surface repair that either returns a closed two-manifold or stops the build. Tetrahedralization is followed by a quality optimization that accepts a vertex move only when the worst element improves. For the finite-element solves the meshes were rebuilt at a node target fixed in advance (77,523, the middle of three), which gives 64 to 133 thousand nodes per heart. The images come from the Sunnybrook Cardiac MR Database, as 256 × 256 short-axis stacks of 8 to 14 slices per patient. §1.3 §4.1–4.3 §4.4 §3.1

Fibres and tissue

Heart muscle is wound in a helix whose angle rotates through the thickness of the wall, and that winding determines how the wall carries load. To reproduce it, the pipeline solves a heat-like equation from the inner surface to the outer one and uses the result to give every element a fibre direction. Each element is then labelled as healthy muscle, border zone or scar.

Check
117.2 ± 3.1° of rotation through the wall, against a 120° target
Tissue
72.2% healthy, 20.6% border zone and 7.22% scar across the ten-patient build

§5.2–5.3

For specialists

A Laplace–Dirichlet transmural coordinate φ, which runs from 0 at the endocardium to 1 at the epicardium, drives a Streeter rule in which the helix angle goes from +60° to −60° and the sheet angle from −65° to +25°. Core scar takes circumferential collagen and the border zone a halved rotation, and orthonormality is verified element by element to 10−9. Because the public dataset contains no late-gadolinium scar imaging, scar and border zone are inferred from regional wall thinning. §2.7 §5.4

Where to inject

The textbook formula for wall stress treats the heart as a thin, uniform sphere, which it is not. The pipeline instead measures the true curvature at every point on the inner wall and corrects the stress estimate with it, and it measures distance along the curved wall instead of in a straight line. Candidate sites are then scored for high stress, for lying in the border zone and for their spacing from sites already chosen, while scar itself is ruled out.

Sites
17 across ten patients, with 82.4% in the border zone and none in scar
Stress
The spherical formula understates border-zone stress by 15 to 40%
Distance
Paths along the wall are 12 to 38% longer than straight lines

§6

For specialists

A least-squares shape operator at each endocardial vertex gives the principal curvatures, and an anisotropy factor bounded to [1, 2.5] modifies the Laplace estimate so that it recovers the classical law on a sphere. Geodesic distance comes from the heat method in three sparse solves that reuse the Laplacian already assembled for the transmural coordinate, and selection is greedy over a three-term score with a minimum geodesic separation. §2.8 §2.9

Fit to the patient

A mesh has a shape but not yet a behaviour. To give it one, the pipeline first recovers each ventricle’s unloaded shape and then tunes how strongly the muscle contracts until the model reproduces that patient’s own imaged volumes. Before any patient was simulated, the muscle model itself was tested against problems that have exact answers.

Design the gel

HYDRA-BERT is the AI model introduced in this work. It reads a polymer’s chemical structure much as a language model reads a sentence, combines it with a description of the patient’s heart, and predicts how that gel would change that heart. A reinforcement-learning policy uses those predictions to propose new formulations, and a six-stage cascade then narrows ten million candidates to a single design that a catheter can deliver.

Model
1.18 million of 86.1 million parameters are trained
Search
107 candidates per patient, reduced to one
Rule
A design that cannot be delivered is rejected and counted, never adjusted to fit

§10.3–10.5 §9.3

For specialists

The encoder is polyBERT, a twelve-layer DeBERTa-v2 pre-trained on polymer SMILES, which supplies a 600-dimensional fingerprint. Low-rank adaptation (rank 16, scaling 32) is applied to the query and value projections of all twelve layers, and the fingerprint is fused with nineteen numerical features. Proximal policy optimization searches a hybrid action space that is discrete in polymer and coverage and continuous in modulus, degradation half-life, conductivity and thickness. The reward penalizes predicted toxicity, fibrosis and loss of structural integrity as step functions, so that predicted benefit cannot carry a design past a safety threshold. Delivery is modelled as discrete mid-wall depots: at least 8 of them, 0.27 to 0.30 mL each and 5 to 10 mm apart, in wall at least 6 mm thick and 2 mm clear of either surface. §10.3 §10.3–10.4 Table 6

Seal, then solve

A fast model is only useful if its choices hold up under full physics. For this test a second, physics-based surrogate ranks catheter-deliverable designs for each patient by their predicted effect on end-systolic volume. Its predictions, their uncertainties and the analysis plan were written to a file and cryptographically hashed before a single verification simulation existed, and only then were the designs solved in full, including the ones predicted to do worst.

The designs in this test are drawn from the deliverable design space, and solving HYDRA-BERT’s own selections in the same way is the first of the next steps.

Each of the 60 models was treated with the formulation HYDRA-BERT selected for it and then compared with its own untreated state. Ejection fraction rose by an average of 9.1 points, stress in the border zone fell by 30.1 percent, and strain across the wall became 17.3 percent more uniform. In the two models that received electrically conductive gels, conduction through the border zone sped up from 28.3 to 48.2 cm/s and the arrhythmia-risk score fell from 0.60 to 0.08.

The search did not collapse onto a single answer either. Six distinct polymer chemistries were chosen across the ten patient-derived hearts, with stiffness settling between 14.9 and 16.2 kPa and degradation half-lives of 45 to 85 days.

What the simulations revealed

Three lessons for designing the next generation of gels

1Predict the change, not the raw volume

Designs for one patient differ by about a millilitre, while patients differ from one another by one to two orders of magnitude more. A model trained to predict absolute volume therefore learned to recognize the patient and could not tell designs apart. Retrained to predict the change from each patient’s own untreated simulation, with the same features and solver, it ranked designs at a held-out correlation of 0.9517, at the cost of one extra simulation per patient.

3What limits today’s catheter dose, and how to get past it

At the small volume a catheter can place today, the modelled effect on ejection fraction was modest, a change of −0.15 to −0.73 points, and the simulations explain why. In the model a gel depot is as incompressible as the muscle around it, so it takes up room that the wall needs in order to thicken, and raising the stiffness five-hundred-fold recovers almost all of that cost. This points directly to the next design target, which is a gel softer in bulk than the tissue it sits in, and it is a prediction that the next round of simulation can test. §11.7 §11.8 IEEE §IV

Patient by patient

Every heart gets its own plan

Because the pipeline works from each patient’s own anatomy, it can report how much gel that anatomy will take before anything is injected. Six of the eight personalized hearts went on to receive a full set of verified designs. For the other two it showed in advance that the border zone was too thin to hold the minimum of eight depots, or that only one layout would fit, a layout the ranking model has not yet been trained to score. Why it matters

Heart failure after a heart attack is common and progressive, and today’s treatments cannot restore the muscle that was lost. This work is a step toward treating it one patient at a time.

  1. Support the injured heart

    Bracing the thinned, overstressed wall could slow or even prevent the slide into heart failure. Wall stress is what feeds the loop of stretching and thinning, and in the HYDRA-BERT evaluation the selected gels lowered it by 30.1 percent in the border zone, where the surviving muscle is under the most strain. Table 7 §12.3

  2. A path toward regeneration

    Stabilizing the wall is the first requirement for anything that hopes to rebuild it. The same patient-tuned gels could one day carry cells or growth factors that help lost muscle regrow, and the design space already includes conductivity and degradation time so that those properties can be tailored to each heart as well. §12.3 §14

  3. Precision care at scale

    The whole route from one MRI to one personalized therapy plan takes about 37 minutes of computing. With roughly 805,000 heart attacks a year in the United States alone, an approach that can be run for every patient could change the outlook for millions of people over time. §10.6 IEEE §I

Next steps

The path from here to a proof of concept has two parts, deeper simulation and first experiments in the lab. Both are ready to begin with the right support.

  1. 1

    Deeper simulation

    The first step is to run HYDRA-BERT’s chosen gels through full finite-element simulation on the personalized hearts, at the dose a catheter can deliver, so that the predicted benefit is confirmed directly. From there the simulations can grow to cover more patients, scar measured by contrast imaging, a complete heartbeat, and gels that are softer in bulk than tissue.

The paper

Abstract

Post-infarction heart failure is driven by a self-reinforcing cycle of wall thinning, wall-stress elevation and chamber dilation. Injectable hydrogels address the structural deficit directly, yet clinical translation is bottlenecked by three convergent limitations: patient-specific, simulation-ready cardiac models are not routinely generated from sparse atlas data without manual intervention; injection sites are placed without a biomechanically principled, manifold-aware rationale; and the combinatorial hydrogel design space is intractable against direct patient-specific simulation. This work develops an eight-stage pipeline converting short-axis cine images and their expert contours into simulation-ready tetrahedral models with zero inverted elements, Laplace–Dirichlet fiber assignment reproducing 117.2 ± 3.1° of transmural rotation and a three-zone tissue classification. On that substrate a targeting method places injection sites by curvature-corrected wall stress and heat-method geodesic spacing. The formulation space is searched by HYDRA-BERT, a model introduced here that couples a low-rank-adapted chemical language model to a reinforcement-learning policy. The Holzapfel–Ogden implementation reproduces closed-form solutions to 4.609 × 10−12 kPa, matches an independent re-implementation to 6 × 10−7 and is loading-path independent in cavity volume to 0.0001 percent. Eight ventricles were personalized to their imaged volumes measured over each mesh’s own slice extent, matching end-diastolic volume to 1.34 percent and end-systolic volume to 1.67 percent. HYDRA-BERT searched 107 formulations per patient at 2.2 × 105 times the throughput of direct simulation, reaching a hold-out R2 of 0.89 and an area under the curve of 0.92. In the HYDRA-BERT evaluation over the augmented cohort of sixty models, ten patient-derived and fifty synthetic, each treated model paired with its own untreated evaluation, the selected formulations gave a mean ejection-fraction improvement of 9.1 ± 0.4 percentage points (p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 1.87 as the mean difference over the pooled standard deviation) and a border-zone wall-stress reduction of 30.1 ± 0.4 percent, each dispersion a standard deviation over models. A separate finite-element program verified the numerical method and a design ranking. That ranking came from a physics-feature Gaussian-process surrogate, a distinct model from HYDRA-BERT. Under nested leave-one-patient-out that surrogate ranked designs at held-out Spearman ρ = 0.9517, and over 210 converged solves its sealed predictions agreed with outcome within patient at 0.929 to 0.983, including 0.981 and 0.983 on two patients absent from training, pooling to 0.765.

Keywords patient-specific cardiac modeling; injectable hydrogel; myocardial infarction; Holzapfel–Ogden constitutive model; finite-element verification; Gaussian-process surrogate; inverse design; chemical language model.
