Computational cardiology / Research by Krishiv Potluri

A second beat for the broken heart

AI-driven inverse design of bio-instructive hydrogels for post-infarction myocardial regeneration

A heart attack leaves scar where muscle was. This work takes a patient’s cardiac MRI and builds a simulation-ready model of their left ventricle. A chemistry model then searches ten million injectable-gel formulations for one predicted to support the weakened wall. Separately, a second model ranks designs a catheter can deliver, and that ranking is checked against direct finite-element simulation.

A computational study. It shows a modelling pipeline and a checked design ranking. It does not show regeneration or clinical benefit.

By Krishiv Potluri, independent researcher, Austin, Texas

01

The heart does not repair itself

  1. A working ventricle

    This is the left ventricle seen in cross-section, the way a short-axis MRI slice shows it. With each beat the wall thickens and the cavity shrinks. Healthy muscle, relaxed between beats, is soft: 8 to 15 kPa. §1.1

  2. A heart attack kills part of the wall

    A blocked coronary artery starves a region of muscle. The cells die and collagen scar replaces them. The scar stiffens to at least 55 kPa. In the model it develops no active tension. About 805,000 heart attacks occur in the United States each year. §1.1 §2.11 IEEE §I

  3. The muscle does not grow back

    The adult heart renews less than one percent of its muscle cells per year. Current drugs slow what happens next. They cannot replace what was lost. §1.1, citing Bergmann et al., 2009

  4. Then the damage feeds itself

    The scar thins and the chamber dilates. By the law of Laplace, wall stress rises with radius and falls with thickness, so both changes raise the stress on the surviving muscle, and raised stress drives more dilation.

    σ = P r2 h

    Among survivors, 20 to 35 percent develop heart failure within one year. §1.1, citing Jenča et al., 2021

  5. One idea: thicken the wall

    Inject a hydrogel into the wall and h goes up, so by the same formula stress should come down. A 2006 simulation found that material filling 4.5 percent of wall volume cut border-zone fibre stress by 17 percent, from 28.2 to 23.3 kPa. §1.2, citing Wall et al., 2006

    Gels can also carry bio-instructive properties: conductivity, a degradation schedule, and the stiffness the material presents to cells. §1.2 §12.3

  6. Why results in patients have been mixed

    Gels have been delivered to patients, and the results are mixed. A randomized trial of a bioabsorbable matrix, delivered through the coronary artery after large heart attacks, did not reduce remodeling against placebo. §1.2, PRESERVATION-1

    Three things stand between the idea and a prescription for one patient. Simulation-ready heart models are not generated routinely from sparse atlas data without manual work. Injection sites are chosen by eye, twelve to twenty of them, with no mechanical rationale. And with a dozen interacting formulation variables, the design space is too large to simulate directly. §1.3

    This work addresses all three.

Six stages, from MRI to a checked ranking

Five stages run from image to a selected formulation. A sixth runs beside them: a test of a design ranking by direct finite-element solves, with the predictions sealed (written to a file and hashed) before any of those solves ran. Select a stage.

Image to model

The input is a stack of short-axis MRI slices with the inner and outer heart wall traced by an expert. Eight automated stages turn those sparse contours into a watertight surface and then into a solid mesh of tetrahedra that a physics solver can use. No one edits the mesh by hand.

Built
10 patients; 3,658,183 elements and 677,557 nodes in total
Check
Zero inverted elements in every mesh
Time
About fifteen minutes per patient

§4.4

For specialists

Cubic Hermite atlas fits of roughly sixty nodes in prolate spheroidal coordinates are evaluated to a point cloud, triangulated, refined by three rounds of Loop subdivision, smoothed, and passed through a seven-operator surface repair that returns a closed two-manifold or aborts the build. Tetrahedralization is followed by a quality optimization that accepts a vertex move only if the worst element improves. The meshes were then rebuilt for the finite-element solves at a node target fixed in advance: 77,523, the middle of three. The rebuilt meshes hold 64 to 133 thousand nodes. Data: Sunnybrook Cardiac MR Database, 256 × 256 short-axis stacks, 8 to 14 slices per patient. §1.3 §4.1–4.3 §4.4 §3.1

Fibres and tissue

Heart muscle is wound in a helix that rotates through the wall, and that winding sets how the wall carries load. The pipeline solves a heat-like equation from the inner surface to the outer one and uses it to assign a fibre direction to every element. It then tags each element as healthy, border zone, or scar.

Check
117.2 ± 3.1° of rotation through the wall, against a 120° target
Tissue
72.2% healthy, 20.6% border zone, 7.22% scar on the ten-patient build

§5.2–5.3

Stated limit. The public dataset has no scar imaging, so scar is inferred from where the wall is thin. The paper calls this a weak inference. §5.4

For specialists

A Laplace–Dirichlet transmural coordinate φ (0 at endocardium, 1 at epicardium) drives a Streeter rule: helix angle from +60° to −60°, sheet angle from −65° to +25°. Core scar takes circumferential collagen and the border zone a halved rotation. Orthonormality is verified element by element: the worst deviation must stay below 10−9 or the build raises an error. §2.7

Where to inject

The textbook stress formula assumes the heart is a thin, uniform sphere. It is none of those. The pipeline measures the real curvature at every point of the inner wall and corrects the stress estimate with it. It measures distance along the curved wall, not in a straight line. Then it scores each site for stress, for lying in the border zone, and for distance from sites already chosen, and picks them one at a time. Scar is excluded outright.

Sites
17 across ten patients; 82.4% in border zone, 0 in scar
Stress

Distance

§6

For specialists

A least-squares shape operator at each endocardial vertex gives the principal curvatures; an anisotropy factor bounded to [1, 2.5] modifies the Laplace estimate and recovers the classical law on a sphere. Geodesic distance uses the heat method in three sparse solves that reuse the Laplacian already assembled for the transmural coordinate. Selection is greedy over a three-term score with a minimum geodesic separation. §2.8 §2.9

Fit to the patient

A mesh has a shape but not yet a behaviour. The pipeline recovers each ventricle’s unloaded shape, then tunes how hard the muscle contracts until the model matches that patient’s imaged volumes over the slices the mesh covers. Before any patient was solved, the muscle model was tested against exact solutions.

Design the gel

HYDRA-BERT is the model introduced in this work. It reads a polymer’s chemical structure the way a language model reads a sentence, combines it with the patient’s cardiac state, and predicts how that gel would change that heart. A reinforcement-learning policy uses those predictions to propose formulations. A six-stage cascade then cuts ten million candidates to one, and that one must be something a catheter can place.

Model
1.18 million of 86.1 million parameters trained
Search
107 candidates per patient, reduced to 1
Rule
An infeasible design is rejected and counted, never adjusted to fit

§10.3–10.5 §9.3

For specialists

The encoder is polyBERT, a twelve-layer DeBERTa-v2 pre-trained on polymer SMILES, giving a 600-dimensional fingerprint. Low-rank adaptation (rank 16, scaling 32) is applied to the query and value projections of all twelve layers. The fingerprint is fused with nineteen numerical features. Proximal policy optimization searches a hybrid action space: discrete polymer and coverage, continuous modulus, degradation half-life, conductivity and thickness. The reward penalizes predicted toxicity, fibrosis and loss of structural integrity as step functions, so predicted benefit cannot buy a design past a safety threshold. Delivery is modelled as discrete mid-wall depots: at least 8, 0.27 to 0.30 mL each, 5 to 10 mm apart, in wall at least 6 mm thick, 2 mm clear of either surface. §10.3 §10.3–10.4 Table 6

Seal, then solve

This stage tests a different model from HYDRA-BERT. A separate physics-based surrogate ranks catheter-deliverable depot designs within each patient by their predicted change in end-systolic volume. Those predictions, their uncertainties and the analysis plan were written to a file and hashed before any verification simulation existed. Only then were the designs solved in full, the predicted-worst included.

The designs solved here are sampled from the deliverable design space. They are not the formulations HYDRA-BERT selected in stage 5.

What the work shows

The paper reports two programs and keeps them apart. So does this page. One is the HYDRA-BERT evaluation, which is fast and runs on surrogate models. The other is a set of direct finite-element solves, which is slow and checks a design ranking made by a separate surrogate.

How to read these numbers

  • They come from forward evaluation by physics-based surrogate models, not from direct simulation of each design.
  • Of the 60 models, 10 are patient-derived and 50 are synthetic draws from a distribution fitted to those ten. Ten is the number of independent anatomies.
  • They are predictions on models. Nothing here has been synthesized or tested in a lab or in a patient.

Finite-element program / direct solves at the catheter dose

At the catheter dose, direct solves found a small, mostly unfavourable effect, and a testable account of why

Solved directly on six personalized ventricles, at the dose the registered catheter route allows, the gel’s effect was small and mostly unfavourable. Ejection fraction fell in all 210 verification arms. End-systolic volume fell in 14 of them. The paper makes no claim of functional improvement from this program. §11.7

The proposed mechanism. In the model, a gel depot is given the tissue’s bulk modulus, so it is as incompressible as the muscle around it. To eject blood the wall must thicken, and the depot gets in the way. A gel with almost no stiffness still raised end-systolic volume by 0.613 to 1.474 mL. Raising stiffness five-hundred-fold removed almost all of that penalty and brought the change below zero, the favourable direction on this target, in 4 of 6 patients. In all six, the change in end-systolic volume fell at every step up in stiffness: rank correlation exactly −1. §11.8

The comparison. Against a clinical-style 4 kPa alginate on the same route, the optimized design (drawn from this program’s own design space, not a HYDRA-BERT selection) had the lower change in end-systolic volume in all six patients, by an estimated 0.817 mL (exact p = 0.03125, the smallest value possible with six patients). It was worse than the stiffest registered gel, 50 kPa, by 0.056 mL at the same exact p. Less harm is not benefit: the top-ranked design lowered end-systolic volume in 2 of 6 patients. The test that would prove it wrong. Give the depot a bulk modulus well below the tissue’s. If volume displacement is the mechanism, the penalty must fall with it. That experiment has not been run. §11.8 §13

Two findings that reach beyond this therapy

Train on the change from each patient’s own baseline. Check first that a design can be delivered.

1. The training target decided whether designs could be ranked at all

Designs for one patient differ by about a millilitre. Patients differ from each other by one to two orders of magnitude more. A surrogate trained to predict absolute volume learned to recognize the patient and could not order designs in any fold. Trained on the difference from that patient’s own untreated solve, with the same features and solver, it ranked them at a held-out rank correlation of 0.9517. The change cost one extra simulation per patient.

2. Whether a design can be delivered is itself a result

Two of eight personalized ventricles could not receive a deliverable, scoreable design, for two different reasons. In one the border zone is too thin: it holds 2 depots against a minimum of 8, and that thickness can be measured from the same images the model is built from. The other admits only a layout the ranking surrogate has too few examples to score. No property of any material enters either case. In the paper’s words, a system that answers “this patient cannot receive this design space” is more useful than one that returns an efficacy estimate for a design that cannot be placed.

Limits that apply to every result above

What this work does not establish

  • All results are computational. Nothing is validated in vitro or in vivo, and no formulation has been synthesized.
  • Scar is inferred from wall thinning. The database has no scar imaging.
  • One public, single-centre database; one expert observer’s contours; eight personalized ventricles, six with a design. No between-patient claim is available.
  • Only peak active tension is fitted per patient. Passive stiffness is the published set, unscaled, for everyone.
  • The models hold one instant. There is no growth or remodeling law, so the remodeling the therapy targets lies outside them.
  • End systole is solved with the whole muscle activated at once. A full heartbeat has not been validated.
  • The chosen mesh passes an agreement test against the finest of three. No resolution is shown to be converged in the strict sense.
  • The two programs are not on a common footing: different cohorts, forward models, doses and conventions.

The paper

Abstract

Post-infarction heart failure is driven by a self-reinforcing cycle of wall thinning, wall-stress elevation and chamber dilation. Injectable hydrogels address the structural deficit directly, yet clinical translation is bottlenecked by three convergent limitations: patient-specific, simulation-ready cardiac models are not routinely generated from sparse atlas data without manual intervention; injection sites are placed without a biomechanically principled, manifold-aware rationale; and the combinatorial hydrogel design space is intractable against direct patient-specific simulation. This work develops an eight-stage pipeline converting short-axis cine images and their expert contours into simulation-ready tetrahedral models with zero inverted elements, Laplace–Dirichlet fiber assignment reproducing 117.2 ± 3.1° of transmural rotation and a three-zone tissue classification. On that substrate a targeting method places injection sites by curvature-corrected wall stress and heat-method geodesic spacing. The formulation space is searched by HYDRA-BERT, a model introduced here that couples a low-rank-adapted chemical language model to a reinforcement-learning policy. The Holzapfel–Ogden implementation reproduces closed-form solutions to 4.609 × 10−12 kPa, matches an independent re-implementation to 6 × 10−7 and is loading-path independent in cavity volume to 0.0001 percent. Eight ventricles were personalized to their imaged volumes measured over each mesh’s own slice extent, matching end-diastolic volume to 1.34 percent and end-systolic volume to 1.67 percent. HYDRA-BERT searched 107 formulations per patient at 2.2 × 105 times the throughput of direct simulation, reaching a hold-out R2 of 0.89 and an area under the curve of 0.92. In the HYDRA-BERT evaluation over the augmented cohort of sixty models, ten patient-derived and fifty synthetic, each treated model paired with its own untreated evaluation, the selected formulations gave a mean ejection-fraction improvement of 9.1 ± 0.4 percentage points (p < 0.0001, Cohen’s d = 1.87 as the mean difference over the pooled standard deviation) and a border-zone wall-stress reduction of 30.1 ± 0.4 percent, each dispersion a standard deviation over models. A separate finite-element program verified the numerical method and a design ranking. That ranking came from a physics-feature Gaussian-process surrogate, a distinct model from HYDRA-BERT. Under nested leave-one-patient-out that surrogate ranked designs at held-out Spearman ρ = 0.9517, and over 210 converged solves its sealed predictions agreed with outcome within patient at 0.929 to 0.983, including 0.981 and 0.983 on two patients absent from training, pooling to 0.765.

Keywords patient-specific cardiac modeling; injectable hydrogel; myocardial infarction; Holzapfel–Ogden constitutive model; finite-element verification; Gaussian-process surrogate; inverse design; chemical language model.

  1. Shown now

    The route from image to a checked design ranking runs end to end

    • Cine MRI with expert contours becomes a simulation-ready, personalized left-ventricle model with no manual mesh editing. Ten of twelve cases were meshed and eight were personalized.
    • A physics-based surrogate, separate from HYDRA-BERT, orders deliverable designs within a patient. That ordering held in sealed finite-element tests on two patients it never trained on (0.981 and 0.983). The predicted level did not transfer.
    • Two of eight personalized ventricles admitted no deliverable, scoreable design under the registered catheter route. For one, the reason is border-zone thickness, measurable from the same images before any gel is chosen.
    • The mechanics is checked against closed-form solutions and a second implementation, and the design ranking against solves whose predictions were sealed first. HYDRA-BERT’s selected formulations have not yet been re-solved directly on the personalized models at the catheter dose.

  2. Next few years

    From a ranking to a prescription someone can test

    • Put the two programs on one footing, so the predicted benefit is confirmed or corrected by direct simulation.
    • Run the experiment that could falsify the mechanism: give the gel a bulk modulus below the tissue’s. If the account is right, the volume penalty falls.
    • Move from eight ventricles to cohorts with real scar imaging, where a between-patient claim becomes possible.
    • Synthesize the leading formulations and measure them on the bench.

    Each of these is named in the paper as work not yet done. §13 §13, continued IEEE §IV

  3. Vision, not shown

    Design materials that help the heart rebuild

    The paper’s direction is “mechanical first.” In its account, relieving stress in the infarct and its border zone interrupts the loop between wall stress and dilation and preserves the chamber geometry an implanted material sits in. That is the condition the material’s bio-instructive properties need in order to act at all. In this paper’s own direct solves at the catheter dose that relief was partial: at end systole, border-zone fibre stress fell in 4 of 6 patients and remote stress rose in all 6. §11.10

    Hydrogels can carry more than stiffness: electrical conduction, a degradation schedule, cells, drugs, support for new blood vessels. The mechanics in this paper has no term for those. The design space already has axes for conductivity and degradation for that reason.

    The long-term aim is a design loop that treats those biological properties the way this work treats mechanics: specific to one patient, checked before anything is injected. This paper assembles the computational groundwork such a therapy would need. It does not show regeneration. §12.3 §14

What a fellowship would let me do

Every step marked “from the paper” is a gap the paper itself names. Steps marked “extension” are my plan beyond it. Select a step.

  1. Phase 1 Close the loop

    1. Re-solve the formulations HYDRA-BERT selected with full finite-element simulation, on the personalized patient models, at the catheter dose, in the matched-pressure convention. This is the comparison the paper says is needed to put its two programs on a common footing. It either confirms the surrogate-predicted benefit or shows where the surrogates are wrong.

      §13

    2. Give the gel inclusion a bulk modulus well below the tissue’s and repeat the stiffness series. If the volume-displacement account is right, the penalty at the volume control falls. If it does not fall, the account is wrong and the paper says so in advance.

      §11.8

    3. The sealed predictions in this work were hashed internally and not lodged with an external registry, and the repositories are not yet public. The next round of predictions will be registered outside the project before any solve runs, and the code released with the records needed to regenerate each table.

      §13 §15

  2. Phase 2 Make the model more real

    1. Replace scar inferred from wall thinning with scar measured by late gadolinium enhancement, using public infarct-segmentation datasets and automatic segmentation to scale beyond one database and one observer. Eight ventricles support a within-patient ranking claim. They do not support a between-patient efficacy claim.

      §3.1 §3.2 §13

    2. Validate a full cardiac cycle with an electrophysiologically derived activation sequence and coupled ejection, and personalize the afterload. Resolve the border-zone conduction calibration, which currently blocks, so that conduction and arrhythmia outcomes can be measured by the finite-element program and not only predicted.

      §11.13 §7.4 §2.13

    3. The current models describe a mechanical state at one moment. They contain no growth or remodeling law, so the evolution the therapy is meant to change lies outside them. Adding one, together with gel degradation over time, is what turns a stress snapshot into a prediction of remodeling.

      §13

  3. Phase 3 Toward the bench, and toward regenerative design

    1. No formulation proposed in this work has been synthesized or tested, and the predicted toxicity, fibrosis and integrity values are model outputs. With a wet-lab partner, synthesize the top candidates and measure stiffness, degradation and conductivity against the values the model assumed.

      §13

    2. In the paper, one of eight personalized ventricles could hold no deliverable design because its border zone was too thin, a quantity measurable from the same images the model is built from. Packaged on its own, that check might indicate whether the registered catheter route is workable for a patient before any material is considered.

      Builds on §12.4

    3. Extend the design space from mechanics toward the properties that could support new tissue: conduction across the scar, a degradation schedule matched to healing, and delivery of cells or drugs. The paper cites these as mechanisms its mechanics has no term for. Giving them terms, each with a check of its own, is the step from modelling mechanical support toward modelling repair.
